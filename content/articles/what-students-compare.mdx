Every spring, a group of students in my program sit down with two or three offers and try to decide what to do with the next several years of their lives. I have watched this happen more times than I can count now, and the thing that surprises firms most is how little the decision has to do with what they emphasized.

Here is what is actually on the table.

## Salary matters, but not the way firms think

Compensation is a threshold, not a ranking. Below a certain number, a student cannot take the job — rent and loan payments are not negotiable. Above that number, an extra few thousand dollars almost never decides anything.

What does decide things is legibility. A student can evaluate a salary. They cannot evaluate "significant growth potential." When one firm quotes a number with a path attached and another quotes a number with adjectives attached, the first one wins even when it pays less.

## The person, not the firm

Students choose a person to learn from. They will tell you they chose a firm, and their LinkedIn will say they chose a firm, but the decision almost always traces back to one conversation with one advisor who took them seriously.

This is why the interview process is the product. A firm that runs a rushed, generic process is telling a candidate exactly what mentorship will feel like there. A firm where a senior advisor spends forty minutes on a candidate's actual questions has already answered the only question that matters.

## Whether the work is real

Ask any student who has had two internships which one they talk about. It is the one where they owned something.

The internship that consists of observing meetings and reorganizing a spreadsheet produces a polite thank-you note and no conversion. The internship where an intern builds a piece of a plan, sits in the meeting where it is presented, and hears a client respond to their work produces a hire.

<Note>
This is the cheapest change on the list. It does not require a new compensation structure or a training budget. It requires deciding that an intern can own one real deliverable, and then supervising it properly.
</Note>

## What the next five years look like

The single most common thing I hear from students weighing offers: "I do not know what I would actually be doing in year three."

Firms rarely answer this, I think because the honest answer is that it depends. But "it depends" reads as "we have not thought about it." Even a rough sketch — here is what an associate does, here is what changes when they pass the exam, here is when they start leading meetings — beats precision that does not exist yet.

## Where postings lose people

A few patterns show up constantly in job listings that students scroll past:

- Titles that describe an org chart rather than a job. Nobody outside the firm knows what a "Tier II Client Service Associate" does.
- Requirements written as a wish list, which filter out the exact candidates who would have grown into the role.
- No mention of the CFP® path, which reads as a firm that does not intend to develop anyone.
- No salary range, which reads as a firm that thinks the number is embarrassing.

None of these are strategic decisions. They are artifacts of a posting someone wrote quickly, four years ago, and has copied ever since.

## The uncomfortable summary

Firms compete on brand, size and sometimes prestige. Students compare mentorship, ownership of real work, and whether the path is written down anywhere.

The overlap between those two lists is smaller than it should be — which is good news, because it means the firms willing to be specific can win talent from firms with far more resources. I have watched exactly that happen. It is not close.
