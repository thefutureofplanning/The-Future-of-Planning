Every profession eventually has to hand itself to the people who come next. Medicine does it through residency. Law does it through a brutal and well-understood apprenticeship. Financial planning has never formalized the step, because for most of its life it did not have to. The people who built this profession built it themselves, out of insurance desks and brokerage floors and a conviction that clients deserved advice rather than product.

That worked beautifully for one generation. It does not scale to the next one.

## The problem is not recruiting

Most conversations about the profession's future begin and end with recruiting. Attract more students. Fund more scholarships. Show up at more career fairs. All of that is good, and I have benefited from every piece of it.

But recruiting is the easy half. The hard half is what happens after someone says yes.

A student who commits to this career is signing up for something they cannot yet evaluate: a decade of technical work whose value only becomes visible late, inside a firm whose economics are usually invisible to them, on a path to ownership that is rarely written down anywhere. We ask twenty-two-year-olds to make a twenty-year bet on a business model they have never seen described.

Some of them make it anyway. The ones who leave are almost never the ones who disliked planning. They are the ones who could not see where the road went.

## What a designed handoff looks like

The firms that do this well are not doing anything exotic. They are being specific in four places.

### Specific about the first year

They define what competent looks like at ninety days, six months and one year — before the hire starts, not during the first review. A new associate should be able to name what they are supposed to be able to do by spring. Vague expectations feel generous and function as a trap.

### Specific about ownership

Not a promise. A path. The associates most likely to stay are the ones who can describe, in one sentence, how someone in their seat comes to own part of this firm. If nobody in the firm can say that sentence out loud, the answer is functionally no.

### Specific about client contact

There is a version of the early-career job that consists entirely of preparation for meetings the associate never attends. It produces excellent technicians who quietly conclude they are not advisors. Contact with real clients, early and supervised, is the training. Everything else is homework.

### Specific about what the firm is for

Advisors under thirty ask a question their predecessors were rarely asked to answer: what is this firm actually trying to be? Not the mission statement. The real answer — who it serves, what it refuses, where it is going in ten years. That answer is a recruiting tool and a retention tool and most firms have never written it down.

<Note label="A caveat">
None of this is a complaint about the generation that built the profession. The firms I admire most were built by people who had no template at all. The point is that improvisation does not transfer. What one founder did by instinct has to become something a twenty-five-year-old can learn on purpose.
</Note>

## Why this is worth getting right

The demand side of this profession is not in question. More households need real planning than there are planners to serve them, and that gap widens every year that a firm decides hiring is too hard.

What is in question is whether the transition happens deliberately or by attrition — whether the profession chooses who inherits it, or simply discovers, one retirement at a time, who happened to still be there.

I have an obvious bias here. I am on the receiving end of this handoff, and I intend to be doing this work for the next fifty years. That is exactly why I would rather it be designed than left to chance.

The firms treating this as a design problem are going to look extremely smart in ten years. Not because they were more generous, but because they were more specific.
