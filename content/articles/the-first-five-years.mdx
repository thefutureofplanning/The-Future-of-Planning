The profession has a retention conversation that is mostly a hiring conversation wearing different clothes. We talk about pipelines, campus outreach and starting salaries, and much less about the years where someone either becomes an advisor or quietly decides they are not one.

Those years are the actual product. Everything before them is marketing.

## What early-career development usually is

In most firms, development happens by proximity. A new associate sits near experienced people, absorbs what they can, asks questions when there is time, and slowly assembles a picture of the job from fragments.

This works, in the sense that some people make it through. It is also enormously wasteful, because the people it works for are the ones who would have made it anywhere — the confident, the well-connected, the ones who already knew what to ask.

The people it fails are the ones who did not know what they did not know.

## Three things that change the odds

### A written scorecard

Not a performance review. A description of what capable looks like at each stage, written before anyone starts. What should an associate be able to do without help at six months? At eighteen? If the firm cannot answer, neither can the associate, and the gap gets filled with anxiety.

### One owned deliverable

Everyone in a firm should have something that is theirs. Not a task they help with — a piece of client work that carries their name inside the firm. Ownership is where technical skill turns into judgment, because it is the first time the consequences of a decision are attached to the person making it.

### A second mentor

The direct supervisor teaches the job. Someone else has to teach the profession — how to think about clients, why the firm does things a certain way, what a career looks like from further along. Firms that assign only one relationship are asking a single person to be both boss and guide, which usually means the second role quietly disappears.

## The exam is not the development plan

Passing the CFP® exam is a milestone, not a curriculum. I have watched firms treat certification as the entirety of their development program, and then wonder why a newly certified associate still cannot run a discovery meeting.

Technical knowledge and advisory capability are related but not the same. One is taught in courses. The other is taught in rooms with clients in them, and it takes longer.

## What good firms do that costs nothing

The best early-career environments I have seen share a habit that has no line item: they explain their reasoning out loud.

Why we recommended that. Why we did not. What we were worried about that the client never saw. Why this meeting went sideways. That narration is the difference between watching planning happen and learning how it works — and it takes a senior advisor about four extra minutes.

## The bet firms are making

A firm that hires an associate is making a bet that this person is worth several years of investment before they contribute proportionally. That bet is standard. What varies wildly is whether the firm does anything to improve its own odds.

The firms losing early-career advisors are rarely losing them to better offers. They are losing them to a slow accumulation of ambiguity — about the path, the standard, and whether anyone is paying attention.

That is fixable. It is mostly a matter of writing things down.
