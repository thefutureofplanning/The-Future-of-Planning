I spent a summer as an intern at a large advisory firm in New York, and I came home with a notebook of observations that did not fit into any of the reflection assignments I was supposed to write.

These are the ones I keep coming back to.

## The meetings before the meetings

The client meeting is the visible part. It is also the shortest part.

What surprised me was the volume of preparation that never surfaces — the internal conversation about what a client actually asked last time, the debate over which of three defensible recommendations to lead with, the decision about what to leave out. By the time a client sits down, most of the real work has already happened in a room they will never see.

Nobody teaches this in a classroom, because it is not knowledge. It is a practice.

## Everyone reads the room

I expected technical brilliance to be the differentiator among the advisors I watched. It was not, mostly because everyone was technically excellent. That was the entry fee.

What separated them was attention. Noticing that a client said "we" for every goal except one. Noticing that the person who asked the most questions was not the person who was worried. Following up on the thing said at minute forty-one rather than the thing said at minute three.

<Note label="What I wrote down that day">
The best advisor in the room was not the one who knew the most. She was the one who remembered what the client had said the previous November, and asked about it before anything else.
</Note>

## Scale is a set of tradeoffs, not a verdict

I arrived with a vague student assumption that big firms are impersonal and small firms are warm. That is not what I found.

A large firm buys specialization: someone whose entire job is tax, someone whose entire job is estate coordination, a service model that does not collapse when one person is sick. It pays for that with layers, process and a slower path to a client relationship of your own.

A small firm buys immediacy and a much shorter path to real responsibility. It pays for that with fragility and with the fact that everyone does everything.

Both are legitimate. Students are rarely shown the tradeoff honestly, which is why so many pick based on brand recognition and then discover they wanted the other thing.

## The interns who converted

There were a number of us. The ones who ended up with offers were not the ones who worked latest or asked the most questions in group sessions.

They were the ones who finished something. A deliverable, start to end, with their name on it internally. It turns out that a firm cannot evaluate enthusiasm, but it can evaluate work — and an intern who never produces anything evaluable is invisible at review time, regardless of how good they were.

## What I would tell a student going in

Three things, none of which I knew in May.

Ask to own one thing early, and be specific about what it is. Take notes on how people talk, not just on what they conclude. And find the person two years ahead of you — not the partner — because they are the only one who can tell you what the next twenty-four months actually feel like.

The summer taught me more about this profession than any semester has. Not because the work was harder, but because it was real, and real work makes an argument that no syllabus can.
