I have watched a lot of software demos for a nineteen-year-old. Most of them are impressive. Almost all of them solve a problem I do not have.

The demos show plans generated faster, scenarios modeled instantly, documents assembled without human assembly. All genuinely useful. None of it touches the part of the job that is actually difficult.

## The hard part was never the math

The hard part is a couple who agree on every number and disagree on every meaning. It is the client who says the goal is retirement at sixty-two and means something else entirely. It is the moment where a recommendation is technically correct and emotionally impossible, and the advisor has to decide which one to solve for.

Software has become excellent at producing the plan. It has not become good at the conversation that determines whether the plan is the right one — because that conversation runs on things a tool cannot observe: hesitation, a glance between spouses, the question someone asks twice.

<PullQuote>
Automation is compressing the parts of this job that were teaching people how to do it.
</PullQuote>

## The real risk is to the training ground

Here is what worries me, and it is not the thing people usually worry about.

Early-career advisors learn by doing the work that is now being automated. Building the cash flow. Reconciling the accounts. Assembling the plan by hand, badly, three times, until the structure makes sense from the inside. That grind was inefficient and it was also the curriculum.

If a firm automates all of it and puts a new associate straight into client-facing work, the associate has better tools and less intuition. They can produce a recommendation without being able to feel when one is wrong.

This is solvable, but only on purpose. A few firms are already doing it — deliberately having associates build things by hand that the software could produce, precisely because the point is the building.

## What I think the next decade looks like

Three predictions I am fairly confident about, and one I am not.

**Confident:** the technical floor rises for everyone. Analysis that was a differentiator in 2015 becomes table stakes. Firms competing on the sophistication of the output will find that competition disappears.

**Confident:** meeting frequency goes up, meeting length goes down. When preparation is cheap, the constraint becomes attention, not production.

**Confident:** the discovery conversation becomes the profession's core skill, and the profession is not currently training for it as if that were true.

**Not confident:** whether clients will pay the same for advice when the artifact is cheap to produce. I hear firm owners assume the answer is obviously yes because the value was always the relationship. I believe that is true. I am less sure that clients have ever been asked to price it that way.

## The part that does not change

Every technology cycle in this profession has produced the same prediction — that advisors are about to be replaced — and the same outcome, which is that advisors ended up doing more of what only people can do.

I expect that again. Not because software is limited, but because the job's center of gravity keeps moving toward the room. The plan gets easier to make. The conversation about what to do with a life does not.

That is a good trade for anyone entering this profession now. The work being automated is the work that was least interesting. What remains is the reason most of us wanted to do this.
